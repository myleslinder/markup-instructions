Why this exists

Bold is what Claude points with

To make something stand out, Claude almost always uses bold, and rarely italic. Bold says a sentence matters, not whether it's a guess, a question for you or something done, so you still read every sentence to sort them.

The usual fix

Instructions can only change the words

Custom instructions are how people shape replies now: an 80/20 rule, a fixed structure, a word limit. Each one works by changing the reply itself, not by showing you which sentence is which.

The idea

Like HTML and CSS, for Claude's replies

Claude already writes its replies in markdown, and already knows markdown's syntax for custom tags, called directives. So a few hundred words of instructions are enough to give it a vocabulary of its own to write, the way HTML gives a page its structure. How each tag looks is a separate choice, and wide open, the way CSS is.

Ideally chat apps would draw directives themselves. They don't yet, so this project includes small renderers for the places it can reach: Claude Code and claude.ai.

Why directives
  • A standard syntax. Directives are a proposed part of markdown, written :name[label]{attributes}, so Claude learned them with the rest of markdown.
  • Any name works. The standard keeps names it doesn't know, so a new tag works before any renderer has heard of it.
  • Readable without a renderer. Where nothing draws them, they read as plain text, the way code reads in a plain editor.

Two halves of a tag

The preset's instructions, in full

They give the reason for each rule rather than examples, so Claude can judge cases they don't name.

On substantial replies, mark key items inline with markdown text directives. The point is to let me find them at a glance, without reading every sentence to find out. Treat the markers as semantic highlighting for prose: a browser extension I use highlights each label by its type, so the marker itself tells me what kind of item it is.
:open-ask[…] needs my input or an action from me before you go on with it.
:open-default[…] a check you leave to me or a call I can override, and the default you'll go on with unless I say otherwise.
:guess[…] you filled a gap or gave your own reading; I haven't agreed it.
:decided[…] I said it or explicitly agreed, whoever first proposed it. Your proposals stay :guess until I agree. Someone else's call I haven't OK'd is :checked (a fact) or :guess (you assume I accept it), not :decided.
:changed[verb …] something you saved, edited, moved or deleted outside the chat this turn. Group alike changes. Name deletes. :changed[nothing] is for the whole turn, when work could have happened but didn't; say what's left undone, or use :open-(ask|default) if it needs me. Changes another session made are :checked, not :changed.
:checked[claim]{src="doc or file"} verified against an internal source (not web results).
IDs ({#ID}) and src work like source citations: they tell me where in the project to find the item, or what you checked it against.
The label is what gets highlighted, so make it the whole claim, or a lead-in to the block it introduces, complete enough to read on its own, and keep reasons and detail outside it. No formatting inside labels. Never put these in code; use backticks to show the syntax literally.
Use them only in your replies to me, never in anything written to be saved, sent or handed on (docs, files, artifacts, drafts, handoff notes), even when that text appears inside your reply.

What it can't fix

Models misreport their own work
A wrong verified mark reads like a badge
Unmarked text may read as settled
Instructions have a cost
What each one means, with sources
  • Models misreport their own work. Agents have claimed tool use they never made and reported success on work that failed (Transluce, 2025; Guo et al., 2025). Marks make those claims easy to find. They don't make them less likely.
  • A wrong verified mark reads like a badge. Citations raised people's trust in answers even when they were random (Ding et al., AAAI 2025). A wrong verified mark may mislead more than the same claim in prose.
  • Unmarked text may read as settled. Tagging some false headlines made untagged ones seem more accurate (Pennycook et al., 2020). What's left unmarked carries an implied status.
  • Instructions have a cost. Heavy format rules can hurt reasoning (Tam et al., 2024). Inline marks are far lighter, and whether they change what a reply says is still untested.
